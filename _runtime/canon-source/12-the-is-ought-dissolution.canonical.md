# The Is-Ought Dissolution

Three centuries of philosophy tripped on a wall built in 1739. Kant tried duty. Mill tried utility. Moore tried intuition. The wall was never a wall — it was a window seen at the wrong time of day. From Principia Mathematica Moralia, Paper 0 — now a canon argument of the framework.

David Hume's is-ought gap is the single most-cited reason the moral and the physical can never be unified: **you cannot derive what ought to be from what is.** Facts don't generate obligations. Every attempt to ground morality objectively has died on this wall for three hundred years — and every serious critic of this framework has it loaded and aimed. This sheet presents the framework's answer: not a bridge across the gap, but the discovery that **there is no gap** — only a tense.

ought(a, φ, t) ≡ misaligned(state(a, t), φ) ∧ consequences not yet arrived

The dissolution in one line: a moral "ought" is the recognition of existing misalignment with invariant structure φ, from the temporal position of an agent who has not yet experienced the consequence. It is not a different category of truth. It is the same truth, time-indexed.

1 · The Wall — What Everyone Got Stuck On

The is-ought gap assumes "ought" is fundamentally _future-tense_. "You ought to do X" implies you haven't done it. That makes ought-claims look like a different species from is-claims: one describes, the other prescribes. Kant answered with duty — the ought smuggled in as a command of reason. Mill answered with utility — the ought smuggled in as a calculation of pleasure. Moore answered with intuition — the ought smuggled in as a simple, unanalyzable property. Every one of them accepted the wall's existence and tried to climb it.

The framework does not climb the wall. It walks around the site and reads the blueprints: the wall was built on a grammatical illusion — the assumption that "ought" names a category of fact rather than a position in time.

2 · The Deployment Argument — Math Doesn't Describe, It Judges

The standard framing says mathematics _describes_ what is. But math does nothing until **deployed**. The equation sits inert until an agent applies it — and the moment of application is the moment of judgment:

Moment| What happens
---|---
**Before deployment**|  The structural violation exists but is invisible. The books are cooked. The bridge is under-engineered. Reality contains the misalignment; nobody has measured it yet.
**At deployment**|  The invariant structure meets the actual state. Deviation becomes visible. The lie is exposed. The error is quantified.
**After deployment**|  Consequences follow. The bridge fails. The fraud is prosecuted. Reality enforces what the math revealed.

Invisible violation → deployment → exposure → consequence. That sequence is not description. It is **judgment**. The truck does not flip because someone violated a moral code; it flips because the load calculation was wrong — and "wrong" here is not a value imported from outside. It is a structural fact about load, axle, center of gravity, and road. The math doesn't care about intentions. It cares about alignment.

And this is exactly what morality does.

3 · The Parallel — Morality as Structural Alignment

Morality, at minimum, is the recognition that some actions produce structural breakdown — of trust, societies, economies, bodies, relationships — and other actions preserve or strengthen them. A lie increases complexity: every lie requires additional lies to maintain, and the informational entropy of a deceptive system rises with each fabrication. Not because a deity intervened. Because structural misalignment accumulates cost.

Test the objection that moral constraints are "subjective preferences": build a society on systematic lying — the result is not a different-but-valid culture, it is collapse. Build a family on betrayal — not an alternative structure, a disintegration. Build an economy on fraud — not differently ordered, failed. **These are empirical regularities with the same shape as physical law: deviation from invariant constraint produces measurable cost.**

4 · The Dissolution — The Ought Is a Tense

Morality is not only future-facing. "That _was_ wrong" is past tense. "This _is_ unjust" is present tense. Both are moral judgments, and both operate exactly like mathematical deployment: apply the invariant structure to the situation and the misalignment reveals itself. Only the "ought" looks different — and it looks different for one reason:

The core move — PMM Paper 0, §4

The "ought" is **what misalignment looks like from the temporal perspective of an agent who has not yet experienced the consequence.** The engineer who hasn't deployed the load calculation feels no urgency — the bridge "seems fine." The moment the math is applied, the misalignment becomes visible. "You ought to reinforce that beam" is simply the recognition of _existing_ misalignment before consequences arrive. After the collapse, nobody says "you ought to have reinforced it." They say "it was wrong." Past tense — and the moral and mathematical judgments become **identical**.

Tense| Form of the judgment| Domain-blind structure
---|---|---
Future ("ought")| "You ought to reinforce that beam."| Misalignment recognized _before_ consequences
Present ("is")| "This is unjust." / "The beam is under-engineered."| Misalignment measured _at_ deployment
Past ("was")| "It was wrong."| Misalignment confirmed _by_ consequence

Therefore: mathematical structure is the invariant against which all states — past, present, future — are measured. Morality is not "what ought to be." Morality is **alignment or misalignment with what is, was, and will be.** The ought was never a separate category. It was "is" viewed from a particular temporal angle, by an agent who doesn't yet see the full structure. The gap dissolves.

6 · The Two Declared Premises — Kept Visible, Per Doctrine

Premise 1 — Universal cost (the Gyges condition)

Every moral misalignment carries structural cost, _even when undetected_. The critic's case is the ring of Gyges — the fraud that is never found. The framework's answer: the violation exists before, during, and after discovery; deployment only reveals it; a deceptive system's entropy rises with each fabrication whether or not anyone audits it. This is defensible — but it is a premise about **universal** cost, and it wears that label. It is falsifiable: exhibit a durably cost-free misalignment and the premise dies.

Premise 2 — Moral ought, not deliberative ought

Prospective choice between two futures neither of which is yet misaligned ("ought I marry?") does not reduce to misalignment-recognition. The framework's line: deliberative ought is not moral ought. The dissolution claims the moral case only. Stated in advance, so no critic gets to "discover" it.

The is-ought gap is not one objection among many. It is _the_ objection — the load-bearing wall between the two halves of the modern intellectual world.
