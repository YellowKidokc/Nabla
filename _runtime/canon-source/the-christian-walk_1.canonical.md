Section I

## Why I Am Sending This

Before we face the question, we face the posture

This is hard for me to write. Not fake hard. Not dramatic hard. Actually hard.

I understand why most Christians do not spend much time with questions like this. I understand why people would rather testify about how good God is, talk about what He has done in their lives, worship Him, serve Him, and move on. There is nothing wrong with that. Not everybody is built to stand between both sides of an argument and feel the full pressure from each direction.

But for whatever reason, I often find myself trying to do exactly that. I do it with science and God. I do it with faith and reason. I do it with Scripture and the hardest objections people raise against Scripture.

And this may be one of the hardest ones.

Part of the reason I care so much about this is because of what I keep seeing happen in online spaces, especially debate rooms where younger people spend enormous amounts of time. The well-informed atheists in those rooms are often extremely knowledgeable. Many of them know the Bible. Many of them know the common Christian responses. Many of them know exactly where Christians are weakest.

What hurts me is not that hard questions are being asked. Hard questions _should_ be asked. What hurts me is watching Christians completely collapse under them.

Most of the Christians who wander into those conversations have never seriously wrestled with these objections. They have never heard the strongest versions. They have never been taught how to think through them. And the first thing they lose is not the argument. The first thing they lose is their posture. They become angry. Defensive. Fearful. Mocking. Proud. Everything Christians are not supposed to become.

I have watched believers who genuinely love God get cornered by questions they were never prepared to answer. And I have watched people use those unanswered questions to walk away from everything — walk away from the God who loved them, walk away from the gospel that saved them, walk away from two thousand years of witness, transformation, sacrifice, and resurrection — because of one passage they could not reconcile.

So I am not writing this because I enjoy sounding like an atheist. I am not writing this because I want to attack Christianity. I am not writing this because I have lost faith.

I am writing this because I think Christians need to learn how to face the strongest version of an objection without falling apart, getting cruel, getting defensive, mocking the person asking, or pretending the question is easy.

In parts of this series, I am going to argue the atheist side as strongly as I can. That does not mean I believe the atheist conclusion. It means I refuse to weaken the objection just so the Christian answer feels easier.

If I soften the argument, I am not doing it justice.

If I make the atheist sound stupid, I am not preparing anyone.

If I dodge the horror, I am not helping anyone become a faithful witness.

And I want to be clear about something: the answer is not only in the information. The answer is in the posture.

The way we respond matters. If we respond with panic, we have already lost something. If we respond with mockery, we have already lost something. If we respond with fake certainty, we have already lost something. If we respond by pretending the verse is not hard, we have already lost something.

The first thing a Christian has to do under a question like this is **remain Christian**. Remain humble. Remain honest. Remain empathetic. Remain reverent. Remain willing to say, "I do not fully know."

That may sound weak to some people. I do not think it is weak. I think it may be the first faithful step.

We are not going to soften the verse.

We are not going to pretend it is easy.

We are not going to use the pain of the text as an excuse to place ourselves above God.

Those three commitments have to stay together.

Section II

## The Verse

Before we talk about answers, we need to understand the question

I want to start with the verse itself. Not a summary. Not an interpretation. Not a defense. The verse.

1 Samuel 15:3

"Now go and strike Amalek and devote to destruction all that they have. Do not spare them, but kill both man and woman, child and infant, ox and sheep, camel and donkey."

Before I go any further, I want to pause.

This section hurts my heart to write. Not because I enjoy asking these questions. Not because I enjoy attacking Christianity. Quite the opposite.

I know that is not where every Christian wants to live. Honestly, I understand why many Christians would rather focus on ministering to people who already want to be ministered to, sharing the gospel plainly, encouraging people, praying with people, and helping them walk with Jesus. That is beautiful work. It matters. I am not taking anything away from it.

But my territory seems to be the hard questions and the hard answers. The place where an objection is strong enough that you cannot dismiss it, but the truth of God is too deep to abandon. The place where you have to hold both sides fairly without letting either side cheat. The place where you refuse to weaken the atheist objection, but you also refuse to take anything away from God's righteousness.

That is the work I seem to be built for.

So if this sounds heavier than a normal family devotional, that is why. I am not trying to make faith colder. I am trying to keep faith honest in the places where people are most likely to lose it.

Some questions should cost us something. Some questions should make us grieve. Some questions should force us to pray.

And maybe that is part of the point.

The goal is not to walk away saying, "That was easy."

The goal is to walk away saying: I still trust God, but now I know how serious the question is.

Section III

## The Objection at Full Force

If we soften the question, we are not really answering it

How do we reconcile the love, grace, mercy, and goodness of God with Old Testament commands where God appears to order the violent destruction of entire people groups?

At first, that question can sound abstract. People might say, "God has the right to judge." And maybe that is true. In fact, many Christians, including myself, believe something very close to that. God is the giver of life. God is the sustainer of life. God is the author of life itself. If every breath ultimately comes from Him, then He has rights over life that no human being possesses.

That is one of the most common Christian answers.

But before we decide whether that answer is sufficient, we should understand exactly what it is trying to solve.

Because we are not just talking about God judging evil in a vague theological sense. We are talking about texts where Israel is commanded to invade, destroy, drive out, and kill. We are talking about war. We are talking about blood. We are talking about cities burning. We are talking about bodies in the streets. We are talking about terrified families running from soldiers. We are talking about screams. We are talking about death.

And not just soldiers.

Women. Children. Infants. Animals. Entire communities. Entire populations.

That is where the question becomes much harder.

If the claim is simply "God judged wicked nations," then someone can ask: What about the children? What about the infants? What exactly had they done? Were they personally guilty? Were they morally responsible for the sins of their culture? Or were they swept into judgment because they were born on the wrong side of a divine land promise?

And if they were swept into judgment, how do we call that good?

**The hard question is not only, "Can God judge?"**

The harder question is: Can God command something that, if any human being commanded it today, we would immediately condemn as cruel, barbaric, and morally horrifying?

If any group today claimed that God told them to enter a land, kill the men, kill the women, kill the children, kill the infants, destroy the animals, and take the land because God had given it to them, we would not hesitate. We would call it religious extremism. We would call it fanaticism. We would call it ethnic cleansing. We would call it genocide. We would call it evil.

So if the answer is yes, God can command what no human being may command, then what exactly do we mean when we say God is good? Because if "good" can include the commanded killing of children, infants, and non-combatants, then the word _good_ starts to feel like it is slipping through our fingers.

That is where the atheist objection gets its force. It is not just saying, "The Old Testament is violent." It is saying:

You Christians condemn child sacrifice among the Canaanites, but then your own Scriptures appear to show God commanding the killing of Canaanite children.

You condemn murder, but then say killing can become holy if God commands it.

You condemn genocide, but then defend conquest when it is Israel doing it under divine authority.

You say God is love, but then you have to explain rivers of blood.

You say God is merciful, but then you have to explain infants under the sword.

You say God is just, but then you have to explain why the guilty and the innocent seem to fall together.

Now imagine standing there. Not as an Israelite. Not as a theologian. Not as someone who already knows the ending of the story.

Imagine standing there as a mother holding a child. Imagine hearing soldiers approaching. Imagine hearing screams from neighboring homes. Imagine hearing steel strike flesh. Imagine watching smoke rise over a city. Imagine watching homes burn. Imagine watching people run. Imagine watching people fall. Imagine knowing that the command was not merely to defeat an army, but to leave nothing alive.

Imagine being the child. Imagine being the infant. Imagine being the family that never heard of Abraham, Moses, David, or Jesus. Imagine being on the receiving end of what the victors later called divine judgment.

That is why this objection has emotional power. It is not merely about theology. It is about human beings. It is about fear. It is about terror. It is about grief. It is about death. It is about blood. It is about whether words like justice, mercy, goodness, and love still mean what we think they mean when they are placed beside scenes of conquest, destruction, slaughter, and what appears to be genocide.

I am not saying there is no answer. I am saying the answer has to be strong enough to face the full horror of the objection.

Because if we soften the question, we are not really answering it.

Section IV

## The Answers Christians Usually Give

Necessary — but not sufficient

Before trying to answer this question, I think it is worth laying out the most common Christian responses. Not caricatures. Not strawmen. The real answers Christians typically give. I want to be fair to each one and also honest about where each one runs into trouble.

#### 1\. God Has the Right to Give and Take Life

God created every human being. Every moment of life is a gift from Him. None of us are owed another day. Because God is the giver of life, He also has the right to take life. Human beings do not have that authority. God does. Many Christians connect this to the idea that every human being has sinned and falls short of the glory of God — no one is morally innocent in the ultimate sense.

**What it gets right:** It establishes a real distinction between God's authority and human authority. Christianity does not teach that human beings may imitate divine judgment whenever they claim God told them to.

**Where it runs out:** The objection is not merely about whether God has authority. It is about how that authority is exercised. Are infants guilty in the same way adults are? This answer may establish God's right to judge. But it does not fully explain why judgment took this particular form.

#### 2\. The Nations Were Exceptionally Wicked

These nations were not ordinary societies. The Amalekites, Canaanites, and others are described as cultures marked by extraordinary evil: violence, idolatry, child sacrifice, sexual exploitation, and deep moral corruption. God was not arbitrarily destroying innocent people. He was judging cultures that had become profoundly wicked. Genesis 15:16 says the iniquity of the Amorites was "not yet complete" — centuries of divine patience before judgment.

**What it gets right:** It prevents the conquest from being framed as random ethnic hatred. Judgment is tied to moral corruption, not racial superiority. Israel itself is later judged when it commits the same kinds of evil.

**Where it runs out:** Even if the adults were guilty, what about the children? What about the infants? What about those who had not personally participated in those practices?

#### 3\. God Was Protecting Israel's Mission

God was preserving Israel from spiritual corruption. Israel was supposed to be the nation through which God's promises would continue — the covenant mission, the line to the Messiah. If Israel absorbed surrounding pagan practices, the redemptive plan itself could be threatened. Later biblical history confirms this: Israel repeatedly falls into idolatry after absorbing practices from neighboring nations.

**What it gets right:** It places the command inside the larger biblical story: Israel, covenant, Messiah, redemption, and the eventual blessing of all nations.

**Where it runs out:** Are we saying children died because they might someday become part of a corrupt culture? Are we saying infants became acceptable casualties because of what their society might eventually produce?

#### 4\. It Was Ancient War Hyperbole

Ancient Near Eastern warfare often used exaggerated language. Kings claimed they destroyed everyone, left no survivors, wiped out entire peoples — even when later records showed those same peoples still existed. Phrases like "leave alive nothing that breathes" may function as military rhetoric rather than literal descriptions. Even within the Old Testament, groups described as destroyed sometimes appear again later.

**What it gets right:** It may reduce the scale of the problem in some passages. It reminds us not to read ancient war rhetoric as if it were a modern military report.

**Where it runs out:** Some texts are very specific. In 1 Samuel 15, Saul is condemned because he did _not_ carry out the command completely. The problem in the story is not that Saul was too violent, but that he was not obedient. Hyperbole may help in some places. It does not solve everything.

#### 5\. The Redemptive-History Argument

Israel occupied a unique place in history. God was establishing a covenant people through whom the Messiah would eventually come. These events were not ordinary wars. They were part of a unique stage in salvation history. The ultimate goal was not conquest — it was redemption. The preservation of Israel eventually led to Jesus. And Jesus eventually brought salvation to the world.

**What it gets right:** It keeps the conquest from becoming a general permission structure for religious violence. No modern nation, church, pastor, prophet, president, or army has the right to use these texts as a license for holy war.

**Where it runs out:** Can a redemptive purpose justify the destruction of non-combatants? Can the road to the Messiah run through commands that look, from the outside, like holy genocide? Can a future good justify present horrors?

I do not think any of these answers, by themselves, completely removes the tension. "God has the right to judge" — true, but that does not answer every moral concern. "Everyone has sinned" — true, but that does not fully explain infants. "The nations were wicked" — true, but that does not fully explain collective judgment. "It was hyperbole" — possible in some cases, but probably not all. "It served redemption" — perhaps, but that still leaves difficult moral questions on the table.

This is not the kind of thing Christians should wave away with "God's ways are higher than our ways." That may be true, but it can also become a shield against moral seriousness.

So where do we go?

We do not go to another quick answer.

We go deeper.

Section V

## Who God Is

Character before crisis

Before we can carry this question, we need to go back to the foundation.

If someone begins with the blood, the slaughter, the children, the horror, and the command, they are already standing inside the hardest part of the storm with no anchor. And without an anchor, the storm defines God for them.

So before we ask, "Why would God command this?" we have to ask a different question entirely.

**Who is God?**

Not merely, "Is God powerful?" Power does not make someone worthy of worship. A tyrant can have power. A demon can have power. An empire can have power. Power alone does not make God.

The God I am talking about is not merely the strongest being in the universe. And that distinction matters more than most people realize, because the entire objection — the entire force of the atheist argument against Old Testament violence — rests on a picture of God that is just power. A cosmic warlord. A celestial dictator who commands death because He can.

If that is who God is, then the atheist is right. That God should be rejected.

But that is not the God of the Bible.

### God Is Love

Not love as sentiment. Not love as emotion. Not love as the warm feeling you get when your children are behaving. Love as the fundamental organizing principle of reality. Love that creates at the cost of vulnerability. Love that gives freedom knowing that freedom will be abused. Love that enters the suffering it could have prevented — not because it was too weak to prevent it, but because prevention would have required destroying the very thing it loved.

God did not create the universe because He was lonely. He created it because love, by its nature, gives. A God who is love and never creates is a contradiction — like a fire that never produces heat. The creation itself is the first evidence of who God is: not power exercised, but love expressed.

### God Is Truth

Not truth as an attribute He possesses, like a tool in a drawer. Truth as His nature. The reason mathematics works. The reason logic holds. The reason the laws of physics do not change from Tuesday to Wednesday. The entire structure of reality — every equation, every constant, every law that governs every particle in every galaxy — rests on a foundation that does not drift.

That matters here because the objection implicitly assumes God might be lying — or at least misleading. The objection assumes that when God says He is good, He might mean something different by "good" than what we mean. But if God is truth, then His words are not arbitrary. When He says He is righteous, He is not redefining the word. He is the reason the word has meaning at all.

### God Is Righteousness

Not righteousness as a rule He follows, as though there were a standard above Him that He is trying to meet. Righteousness as the thing He _is_. The moral structure of the universe is not something God invented. It is something God radiates. The reason injustice feels wrong to every human being everywhere — even when they disagree about the details — is that the moral order is woven into the fabric of what God made, because it is woven into the fabric of who God is.

This means that when God judges, He is not imposing an arbitrary standard. He is describing what happens when reality encounters corruption. The stove does not burn you because it is angry. It burns you because it is hot. That is its nature. And when God's righteousness encounters sin, the result is not arbitrary punishment. It is the natural consequence of corruption meeting incorruption.

### God Is the Source of Life

Every heartbeat. Every breath. Every sunrise. Every cell dividing in every body on the planet. All of it comes from Him. Not in a distant, deistic sense — not a God who lit the fuse and walked away — but in an intimate, sustaining, moment-by-moment sense. The universe does not run on its own. It runs because He holds it. If He withdrew His sustaining presence, reality would not slowly wind down. It would cease to exist.

That changes the frame on death entirely. When we talk about God "taking" life, we are not talking about a bystander who decides to kill. We are talking about the source of every heartbeat deciding how long that heart will beat. Every single death — yours, mine, everyone's — is already in His hands. The question is never whether God has authority over death. The question is what kind of God exercises that authority.

### God Is the One Who Defeated Death

And this is where the picture shifts completely. Because if God were merely the author of life who also permits death, you might still wonder whether He understands what death costs. You might still wonder whether He is distant from the suffering.

But this God did not stay on the throne.

He entered the world He made. He took a body that could bleed. He walked among the people He created. He touched lepers. He wept at graves. He healed the broken. And then He submitted Himself to the worst death the Roman Empire could devise — not because He was too weak to prevent it, but because the cure for death required going through it.

The God who commands judgment is the same God who submitted to judgment. The God who holds authority over death is the same God who died. The God who allows suffering is the same God who suffered more than any human being who has ever lived.

That does not make the Old Testament easy. But it tells you something essential about the heart behind the authority.

### God Can Hold What We Cannot

Stay with me here, because this is the piece most people miss.

No human being can fully hold 100% justice and 100% mercy at the same time.

We can lean toward justice. We can lean toward mercy. We can try to balance them. But we cannot perfectly unify them. Too much justice in human hands becomes cruelty. Too much mercy in human hands becomes permissiveness. Every court, every parent, every government, every relationship you have ever been in — all of them struggle with this tension. We are always choosing which one to compromise.

Only God can hold both without corruption.

That is not a theological platitude. That is the structural reality that makes the entire framework hold. And it is the reason the cross is not just a nice story. It is the only event in history where perfect justice and perfect mercy converged in the same moment, in the same person, without either one being reduced.

So when we reach a passage where justice and mercy seem impossible to reconcile — and 1 Samuel 15 is one of those passages — we should not pretend we can solve it from above. We are not above God. We are under Him.

That does not mean we stop asking. It means we ask from the right place.

If you enter the crisis before establishing the character of God, the crisis defines God for you.

If you establish God's character first, then the crisis becomes something you bring to God instead of something you use to replace Him.

That is what it means to say: **character before crisis.**

Section VI

## The Father Who Knows More

If love deepens with knowledge, then God's love is deeper than ours

There is another part of this I do not want to miss.

When we see death, especially the death of a child, we naturally assume that if someone allowed it, they must not care as much as we do. That instinct runs deep. It feels obvious. If I am horrified and God is not preventing it, then either God does not know, God does not care, or God is not there.

But I do not think that follows.

Imagine a two-year-old child has cancer. She has a six-year-old brother. She has an eighteen-year-old brother. And she has a father.

All three love her. All three would grieve her death.

But who understands the loss the most?

The father.

Not because the brothers do not love her. They do. But the father understands more. He understands life more deeply. He understands death more fully. He understands what cancer means. He understands what she will miss. He understands the future being taken from her. He remembers her birth. He knows her laugh in the middle of the night. He knows her fears. He knows the way she reaches for his hand in a parking lot. He knows her possible future in a way her brothers cannot — because he has lived long enough to know what a life contains.

The six-year-old may cry because his sister is gone. The eighteen-year-old may understand much more. But the father carries the deepest knowledge of what was lost.

And because he knows more, he grieves more.

That matters when we talk about God.

Sometimes we act like our grief over death is evidence that we are more compassionate than God. We see the suffering and we assume our horror proves something — that we care more, that we are more sensitive, that our moral compass is more finely calibrated than the One who made the universe.

But if God is truly God, then He knows every person more deeply than we ever could.

He knows every child completely. Not the way we know a stranger's child whose story we read about — from the outside, with sympathy but without intimacy. He knows them the way the father knows his daughter. He knows every life from the inside. He knows every possible future. He knows every unlived moment. He knows every laugh that will not happen. He knows every relationship that will never form. He knows the full weight of death in a way we cannot.

So if knowledge increases grief, then God grieves death more deeply than anyone.

### The Missing Piece

Here is what most people do not carry far enough.

If God grieves more than we do — and He must, if He knows more — then every judgment in the Old Testament was issued by someone who understood its cost more deeply than any human being ever could. Every death God permitted was a death He felt more acutely than the victim's own mother. Every child who fell was a child God knew by name, by future, by every unlived breath.

That does not make the death clean. But it destroys one thing completely: the assumption that God is cold. The assumption that these judgments were issued from a position of indifference. The assumption that the God who commands is distant from the cost of what He commands.

He is not distant from it.

He is closer to it than anyone.

And if He still judged — knowing the full cost, feeling the full weight, carrying the full grief — then we are dealing with something deeper and more terrifying than human cruelty. We are dealing with a God who knows more than we know, loves more than we love, grieves more than we grieve, and still sees realities of justice and mercy that we cannot fully hold.

This does not solve the hard verse.

But it keeps me from pretending my grief is greater than God's.

It keeps me from saying, "If I care this much, God must care less."

No. If God is Father, then He cares more.

And that means when I bring this question to Him, I am not bringing it to someone cold. I am bringing it to the One who knows the full cost of every death — and who bore the worst one Himself.

Section VII

## The Seat You Are Sitting In

The questioner is not a neutral judge

Before we go to the equation and before we go to the cross, I need to say something that might not feel comfortable.

I have spent the last several sections doing something unusual. I have been arguing the hardest objection I know how to argue against the God I love. I have not softened it. I have not dodged it. I have not pretended the verse is easy or the blood is clean.

And I meant every word of that. The objection deserves its full weight.

But now I need to turn the light around.

Not to attack the person asking. Not to dodge the question. Not to say "well you're bad too, so shut up." That is not what I am doing, and if it ever sounds like that, I have failed.

What I am doing is asking something about the seat the questioner is sitting in.

Because here is the move that almost always happens in these conversations. Someone reads 1 Samuel 15:3. They see the blood. They see the children. They feel the horror. And then they sit down in the judge's chair and say: "I would never do that. Therefore I am morally superior to this God. Therefore this God is either evil or does not exist."

And I understand that move. I feel the pull of it myself. The horror is real. The grief is real. The instinct to protect children is real and good.

But the move has a problem inside it.

The person sitting in that chair believes they are morally clean enough to judge God. They believe the distance between themselves and the horror of that verse is so large that they have earned the right to pronounce verdict.

I want to ask a question about that distance.

What is your moral standard?

If the standard is actions only — what you have physically done with your hands — then maybe you can sit in that chair comfortably. Maybe you have never physically harmed a child. Maybe your record, measured by behavior alone, looks clean enough to judge.

But that is not the standard Jesus taught.

Jesus said if you have hated someone in your heart, you have already committed murder. Jesus said if you have looked at someone with lust, you have already violated them. Jesus said the outside of the cup can look clean while the inside is full of greed and wickedness.

That is a different standard.

And under that standard, the distance between the questioner and the verse starts to close. Not because the questioner killed babies. Nobody is saying that. The point is not equivalence. The point is that the standard the questioner is using to judge God is almost certainly not the standard God uses to measure the questioner.

### The Scientific Parallel

In science, when you find an anomaly — one observation that does not fit your otherwise successful theory — you do not immediately throw out the theory. You investigate. You test. You ask whether the anomaly reveals a flaw in the theory or a gap in your understanding.

What you do not do is pretend the anomaly overthrows everything else you have confirmed across thousands of observations.

That is bad science. Every working physicist knows it.

Now apply the same discipline here.

If someone has encountered the love of God, the truth of Scripture, the transformation of the gospel, the reality of prayer, the witness of creation, the moral order of the universe, the historical resurrection, and the changed lives of millions of people across two thousand years — and then they hit one verse they cannot reconcile — what do they do?

They can do what bad science does: let one anomaly overthrow the entire framework.

Or they can do what good science does: admit the anomaly is real, keep investigating it, and refuse to let one unresolved problem erase everything else they know.

That is not blind faith. That is epistemic discipline.

And if we adopted the rule that one unresolved difficulty invalidates an entire worldview, then nothing survives. Not Christianity. Not atheism. Not science. Not philosophy. Not any relationship you have ever had with another human being. Because every framework, every theory, every person you trust has moments you cannot fully explain.

The question is never whether anomalies exist. The question is whether the anomaly is large enough to overthrow the foundation.

Be careful when you judge God. Not because the question is forbidden. Because you may not be standing where you think you are standing when you ask it.

Section VIII

## The Three-Constraint Equation

Drop any term and it breaks

Every hard Old Testament passage — slavery, stoning, conquest, violence, treatment of women, "angry God" — reduces to one question:

**How can the same God be perfectly just AND perfectly merciful AND preserve free will — simultaneously?**

Four frameworks attempt to answer this. All capture something real. None hold the whole thing.

#### The Atheist Position: "God is not actually good"

Logically clean. Internally consistent. Honest about the difficulty. But it has a fatal flaw: the critic uses a moral standard to judge God, but that standard requires a source. If there is no God, there is no ground for the moral intuition you are using to reject God. You are sawing off the branch you are sitting on.

#### The Reinterpretation Position: "We're misreading the text"

Context genuinely matters. Bronze Age is not 2024. But at some point you are selectively rewriting plain text. If you can explain away the hard parts, what stops someone from explaining away the parts you like?

#### The Sovereignty Position: "God operates on a different moral scale"

The scale difference IS real. Parents do things children cannot understand. But this becomes a blank check. If "good" means "whatever God does," the word has no content. Every atrocity in history has been justified by "God told me to."

#### The Progressive Revelation Position: "God works through broken history"

The trajectory IS real. Always toward more humane. The arrow never reverses. But it does not explain the pace. Why not fix it instantly?

Our framework holds all four truths without dropping any of them. Three constraints. Held simultaneously. No contradiction.

dC/dt = O · G(1−C) − S · C

#### Justice (S·C)

Sin has consequences not because God is vengeful but because entropy is real. Touch the stove, get burned. That is not cruelty. That is physics. God does not IMPOSE consequences. He DESCRIBES them. Sometimes He executes them — the way a surgeon cuts to save, not to harm.

#### Mercy (G)

Grace never dropped to zero. Not once. Not in the flood. Not in the conquest. Not in the exile. Noah was grace. Rahab was grace. The remnant was always preserved. The sacrificial system was grace — a prototype of the cure. Grace was not invented in the New Testament. It was UNVEILED.

#### Free Will (O)

God will not override it. Period. This single constraint explains every "why didn't God just..." question. Preventing suffering in a world of free agents requires removing agency. And removing agency destroys the only thing that makes love possible.

### Applied to the Hardest Questions

#### "Why did God allow slavery?"

**Justice:** The world is broken. People enslave each other. That is entropy in social systems.

**Mercy:** God regulates it — seven-year release, protections, fugitive protection. First legal protections for enslaved people in human history. Floor established, then raised across centuries.

**Free Will:** Abolishing instantly overrides every human choice in the system. God moves the floor upward at the pace the patient can survive.

**The trajectory:** From regulated slavery → "neither slave nor free" (Galatians 3:28). ONE direction. Always.

#### "Why does God command stoning?"

**Justice:** Sin in covenant community has real consequences. The diagnostic is honest.

**Mercy:** Evidentiary requirements so high it almost never happens. Multiple witnesses required. False testimony carries the same penalty. It is a BOUNDARY, not a quota.

**Free Will:** The person chose the action knowing the consequence.

**Context:** Before this law: blood feuds, generational revenge, escalating violence with no endpoint. "Eye for eye" is the first DE-ESCALATION law in human history. Proportional. Stop escalating.

#### "Why did God command the conquest of Canaan?"

**Justice:** Four hundred years of child sacrifice. Burning infants alive in the arms of Molech. Archaeology confirms it.

**Mercy:** God WAITED four hundred years (Genesis 15:16). Four centuries of grace offered. They did not stop.

**Free Will:** They chose. For four hundred years.

**Protection:** If Israel adopts Canaanite practices, child sacrifice enters the lineage. The cure never arrives. Billions lose access to redemption.

#### "Why does God seem different in the Old Testament vs. the New?"

Same God. Same equation. Different terms highlighted.

**Old Testament:** Justice (S·C) dominates the narrative — diagnostic phase.

**New Testament:** Mercy (O·G) dominates the narrative — treatment phase.

Same patient. Same doctor. Different phase of one treatment plan.

"My doctor was mean when he diagnosed me but nice when he treated me." No — HONEST when diagnosing. GENEROUS when treating.

This equation does not explain away every hard passage. That is not the claim. The claim is stronger: **this equation identifies the three constraints that every serious answer must preserve.** Any answer that drops justice, mercy, or freedom becomes false to the biblical God. Any framework that cannot hold all three at once is incomplete — ours or anyone else's.

Build me a better God. I will show you which constraint you dropped.

But if justice AND mercy must be satisfied simultaneously, if both the debt and the debtor must be honored, if the law must hold and the person must survive — then who could possibly pay?

Section IX

## The Story of the King

Three scenes. One king. The whole gospel.

### Act I — From the Throne

There was a king — the only one anyone could remember who had never been caught in a lie, never taken a bribe, and never changed a ruling because it was convenient. His enemies hated him. His people trusted him. And every law he wrote existed for one reason: to protect the life of the kingdom.

A plague swept through the eastern villages. It spread through the wells. The king ordered every contaminated well sealed and the villages quarantined — no one in, no one out, until the sickness passed. Healers were sent. Supplies were rationed. But no one could leave.

The people in those villages screamed. They said he was imprisoning them. They said he did not care. They said if he really loved them, he would let them come to the capital where the medicine was better. Petitions were written. Protests broke out. A mother held up her sick child at the barricade and wept so hard her body shook, and the soldiers who held the line wept with her but did not move.

The quarantine held. The plague burned out in three weeks. The capital never got sick. Thousands lived who would have died.

Half the kingdom called him wise. Half called him cruel. He was the same man in both conversations.

A famine hit the south. The king opened the royal granaries and rationed the grain — every family got the same share, rich and poor alike. The wealthy were furious. They said they had earned more and deserved more. Their tables had always been full; why should they eat the same portions as field workers? The poor said the portions were too small and the king must be hoarding. They could see the granary walls from their windows. How could there not be more? Accusations flew from both sides. He reduced his own household to the same ration as everyone else. His own children went hungry some nights. His youngest daughter asked why they could not eat the bread she could smell baking in the lower kitchens, and he held her and told her that a king who eats while his people starve is not a king.

The famine passed. The kingdom survived intact. No one starved to death.

Some praised him for it. Others never forgave him. He had done the same thing to both groups.

A nobleman's son murdered a merchant's daughter. The evidence was overwhelming — witnesses, weapon, confession. The law demanded execution. The nobleman begged. He offered land, gold, titles, anything. Half the court wanted leniency because the nobleman was powerful and had powerful friends. Half wanted blood because the girl was beloved and her father had served the kingdom his whole life.

The king said, "The law does not ask who your father is."

The son was executed. The nobleman became the king's enemy for life. The merchant's family wept with grief and gratitude in the same breath — grief that their daughter was gone, gratitude that the law had meant what it said. Justice was served. It cost the king an ally and bought him nothing but the knowledge that the law meant what it said.

A border tribe had been raiding the northern settlements for years — burning homes, stealing livestock, dragging people into the hills. The king sent messengers. They returned empty-handed. He sent diplomats with gifts. They returned with threats. He sent warnings with deadlines. The deadlines passed. He sent final warnings. The raids continued. For a decade, he tried everything short of force. Then they burned a schoolhouse with children inside. The smoke was visible from three villages away. The screaming lasted longer than the fire.

He sent the army. The raids ended. The border tribe was scattered. Some of them were innocent. Some of them were children themselves. The kingdom was safe. The king never spoke of it as a victory.

Every decision, the same pattern. No matter what he chose, someone called it unjust. The quarantine was tyranny to the quarantined. The rationing was theft to the rich. The execution was murder to the nobleman. The war was genocide to the scattered.

And every time, the people who disagreed were not stupid. They had reasons. They had pain. They had arguments that made sense from where they were standing.

But none of them were standing where the king stood. None of them could see every village, every well, every granary, every border, every consequence of every choice rippling out across a kingdom of millions. They saw their corner. He saw the whole map.

That is what it looks like to rule justly in a broken world. Not clean. Not popular. Not comfortable. Just — the least amount of destruction that truth will allow.

### Act II — In the Streets

One morning the king left the palace before dawn. He cut his hair. He put on a laborer's clothes — rough wool, cracked leather belt, sandals with no gilding. He walked out through the servant's gate with nothing but a bag and a walking stick.

Nobody recognized him.

He walked into the same eastern villages he had quarantined. The quarantine markers were still on the doors — faded paint, chipped wood. A woman was trying to rebuild a wall that had collapsed during the plague. She had been stacking stones alone for three days. Her hands were raw. He knelt beside her and started lifting the heavy ones she could not move. She gave him water from a clay jug. She told him she had lost her husband during the quarantine — not to the plague, but to despair. He had stopped eating. He had stopped talking. He had laid down one evening and by morning his heart had quit. She said she still set two cups at the table every night because she could not stop.

The king sat with her until she finished talking. He did not say anything wise. He did not explain the quarantine. He did not justify the decision. He just stayed. When she finally stood up to go back to the wall, he stood with her and kept stacking stones until the sun went down.

He walked south into the farmlands where the famine had hit. The soil was still recovering — thin, pale, exhausted from years of giving everything it had. A boy was working a field alone. His parents had died during the shortage — his mother from hunger, his father from a fever that came after the hunger weakened him. The boy was twelve. He had been working the field by himself for seven months. He did not know how to read the soil. He did not know how to fix the irrigation channel that had cracked during the drought. He just kept planting seeds in the same exhausted dirt and wondering why nothing grew.

The king knelt in the dirt beside him. He worked alongside the boy for three days. He showed him how to read the color of the soil — where it was ready, where it needed rest. He fixed the irrigation channel with his own hands, packing the clay and redirecting the water. He taught the boy how to rotate crops so the land could recover. When he left, the boy did not know who had helped him. He just knew someone had.

He found a woman on the road who had been beaten and robbed. Her face was swollen. Her arm was broken. She was lying in the dust in the middle of the afternoon and people were walking past her because the road was busy and nobody wanted the trouble. The king cleaned her wounds with water from his own canteen. He tore strips from his own shirt to bind the broken arm. He carried her to the nearest village on his back — half a mile, uphill, in the midday heat. He paid for her lodging with the little money he had. He asked nothing. He gave his name as common and forgettable.

He walked through the border region where the army had been. The rubble was still there. The burned schoolhouse had not been rebuilt — it stood like a skeleton against the sky, charred beams pointing at nothing. He found families still grieving. He sat with them too. He did not explain the war. He did not justify the decision. He did not say the words "it was necessary" or "the kingdom needed protection." He mourned with the people who had been caught in the middle of a fight they did not start. Some of them cursed the king to his face, not knowing who they were talking to. He let them. He did not flinch. He did not correct them. He just let them pour out their grief against the man they believed had destroyed their lives, and he absorbed every word without defending himself.

Every situation was different. A collapsed wall. A fatherless boy. A beaten woman. A grieving family. And in every single one, the king did the same thing. He showed up. He got low. He served whoever was in front of him. He protected whoever was vulnerable. He loved the people who hated him and the people who loved him with the same hands.

It did not matter what the crisis was. The man was the same in all of them.

From the throne, you saw his justice — and it looked severe.

From the streets, you saw his heart — and it looked like love.

It was the same king the entire time.

### Act III — The Daughter

He returned to the palace. Weeks had passed. The kingdom had carried on without knowing where he had been.

The morning he walked back into the throne room, his enemies were already waiting. They were smiling. Not the smile of men who had good news — the smile of men who had been planning something and had finally seen it come together.

"A thief has been caught," they said. "Stolen from the public treasury. The law is clear. What is the sentence?"

The king looked at the record. The evidence was clean. The theft was confirmed. He did not hesitate.

"The law is clear. One hundred lashes."

"Even for this thief?"

They pulled back the hood.

It was his daughter.

The court went silent. Not the quiet of politeness. The quiet of people watching a man's world collapse. Every eye turned to the king.

His enemies did not care about justice. They cared about watching him break. They had planned this — perhaps they had even arranged it. They wanted to see which gave first: his law or his love. If he released her, his law was meaningless. Every ruling he had ever made could be challenged. The quarantine was just his preference. The execution was just his mood. The rationing was just his power. Everything he had ever stood for would collapse in a single act of favoritism.

But if he held the sentence, his daughter would die. One hundred lashes on the body of a young woman. The math was simple. She would not survive.

The king looked at his daughter. She was trembling. Her wrists were bound. Her eyes were red. She did not deny what she had done.

He stood.

"The sentence stands," he said.

A gasp moved through the room like a wave. His enemies leaned forward. His daughter closed her eyes.

Then the king stepped down from the throne.

He removed his robe. He unfastened his crown and set it on the seat behind him. He walked across the stone floor in his underclothes, barefoot, and stood between his daughter and the executioner. He turned his back to the whip.

"The law demands one hundred lashes," he said. His voice did not shake. "I wrote the law. The price will be paid. Begin."

The executioner's hands shook. "My lord — I cannot strike the king."

"You must. The law does not ask who stands before it. Only that the debt is paid."

The first lash fell. The king did not cry out.

The second. The tenth. The thirtieth. His back opened. Blood ran onto the stone floor. His daughter screamed. He told her not to look away.

The fiftieth. He dropped to one knee. He did not ask them to stop.

The seventieth. The eightieth. The court was weeping. Even his enemies could not watch. The men who had planned this turned their faces to the wall. They had wanted to see him break. They had not expected this.

The hundredth lash fell.

The king collapsed. He did not get up for a very long time.

His daughter lived. The law was fulfilled. The debt was paid in full. Not one lash was forgiven. Not one lash was faked. The full weight of justice fell — on the only person in the kingdom who did not deserve it.

And the kingdom finally understood something they could not have learned any other way:

The king's law was never cruelty. It was the price of holding a kingdom together in a world that tears everything apart. And the king's love was never softness. It was the willingness to pay that price himself rather than let it destroy the person he loved.

Justice and mercy were never enemies.

They met in the same body, on the same floor, under the same whip.

The only one who could satisfy the law was the one who wrote it.

Three scenes. One king.

From the throne — you see his justice, and it frightens you.

From the streets — you see his heart, and it breaks you.

At the whipping post — you see both at once, and you fall to your knees.

That is the God of the Bible.

The King who ruled. The King who served. The King who bled.

Not three different Gods. One God, seen from three places.

And if you only accept the King in the streets and reject the King on the throne, you are not worshipping God. You are worshipping a version of God you control.

If you only accept the King on the throne and reject the King in the streets, you are worshipping power, not love.

The only God worth trusting is all three.

Section X

## The Judge Has Scars

Not an argument. A person. Not an explanation. A cross.

Now I want to address the verse directly.

I do not have a clean answer.

This is one of the hardest things in the Bible for me. Intellectually, emotionally, spiritually, and honestly in my soul, this is difficult. I believe God is righteous. I believe God is 100% justice and 100% mercy. I believe God is love. I believe God defeated death. I believe Scripture is true and that this verse is not something I get to simply erase because it makes me uncomfortable.

But I also cannot pretend this verse is easy.

It is not.

If someone asks me, "How can the God revealed in Jesus also be the God connected to a command like this?" my most honest answer is:

I do not fully know.

I have partial frames. I have theological categories. I can talk about divine judgment. I can talk about ancient war language. I can talk about Israel's unique role in redemptive history. I can talk about the three-constraint equation. I can talk about the cross.

But none of those answers completely removes the weight of the question for me.

So I will not pretend they do.

What I can say is this:

I am not here because I think I am morally better than God. I am not here because I think I can judge the Judge of all the earth from a higher seat than His.

I am here because I know who He is.

I know the God I have met in Jesus Christ. I know the God who forgives sinners. I know the God who touched lepers when no one else would go near them. I know the God who protected the woman caught in adultery — not by denying her sin but by standing between her and the stones. I know the God who blessed children. I know the God who told Peter to put away the sword. I know the God who prayed, "Father, forgive them," while being murdered by the people He came to save. I know the God who died for His enemies.

And there is one more thing I keep coming back to.

We want a king who rules with justice. We want a king who lives with mercy. But in our world, those feel like two different people. The ruler who enforces justice is usually distant from the suffering he causes. He signs the order and goes back to dinner. The one who enters suffering is usually the victim, not the king. He has no authority to change the system that crushed him.

But the God I am talking about is not two different kings. He is one King.

The King who ruled — who holds the right to judge all the earth.

The King who served — who entered human suffering, knelt in the dirt, washed feet, and healed the broken.

And the King who somehow holds perfect justice and perfect mercy together without corruption — something no human court, no parent, no government, no philosopher has ever fully achieved.

I do not know how He does it. I do not know how those three are one. But I know this: if I only accept the King who served and reject the King who ruled, I am not worshipping God. I am worshipping a version of God that I control. And if I only accept the King who ruled and reject the King who served, I am worshipping power, not love.

The only God I can trust is the one who is all three.

### The Cross

The cross is where all three converge.

The God who holds the right to judge did not exercise that right from a distance. He entered the judgment. He absorbed the violence. He took the sword into Himself. He died under the same kind of cruelty He once commanded against wickedness — except this time, the innocent One chose to receive it voluntarily on behalf of the guilty.

Justice: someone has to pay. The cost is real. The law does not bend. The debt does not disappear.

Mercy: God pays it Himself. The cure is free. The debtor survives.

Free will: you have to choose to receive it. The door opens from inside.

All three terms. Same moment. Same event. No contradiction.

The Judge is not sitting in comfort while others bleed. The Judge bled. The Judge has scars. The Judge walked through the worst thing any human being has ever experienced and came out the other side holding resurrection in His hands.

So when I bring this question to God — and I do bring it — I am not bringing it to a distant tyrant. I am bringing it to someone who has been where the victims of judgment are. Someone who knows what it feels like to be on the receiving end of condemnation. Someone who has absorbed more violence than any human being who has ever lived.

### The Deeper Question

And maybe that is part of the point. If God is all-knowing, then He knew this verse would trouble people. He knew skeptics would use it. He knew believers would struggle with it. He knew we would be having this exact conversation right now.

So why leave it there?

I do not know completely.

Maybe because God refuses to give us a Bible that lets us control Him. Maybe because He will not let us reduce Him to a soft, modern, harmless version of love that never costs anything and never demands anything. Maybe because judgment is more terrifying than we want to admit. Maybe because sin is more destructive than we understand. Maybe because mercy means nothing if judgment is not real. Maybe because God wants to separate shallow belief from trust that has actually wrestled with Him. Maybe because He wants us to bring the question to Him instead of pretending we can master Him from the outside.

Maybe the text functions like the tree in the Garden. God could have made a Bible with no stumbling blocks, just as He could have made a Garden with no forbidden tree. But then trust would never be tested. The question becomes: when you hit the hardest wall, do you use it to place yourself above God, or do you bring it to the God you have already met?

### The Final Frame

The arc of the Bible is this: violence and judgment are real. Sin destroys societies. God judges evil. Israel itself fails. The Messiah comes. And instead of killing His enemies, He dies for them. Final judgment belongs to God, not vigilantes or holy warriors.

The cross does not answer every question by explanation.

But it reveals the Judge.

And the Judge has scars.

Every hard Old Testament passage is the logical consequence of a God who refuses to drop justice, refuses to withhold mercy, and refuses to override freedom — all at the same time, for fifteen centuries, while parenting a species that could not understand the word "love" yet.

**Love is calculus and they had not learned to count yet.**

But I know who You are, and I will not put myself above You.

I will hold the tension. I will trust the God revealed in Jesus.
